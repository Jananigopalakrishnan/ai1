Atoms: The Building Blocks Underneath Biology

Before you can understand a single bond, reaction, or molecule in this course, you need to know what's inside the atom itself. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to identify the three subatomic particles, tell isotopes and ions apart, and predict an atom's behavior just by counting its valence electrons.

Before we start

What do you already think?

No grades here — just a gut-check. Answer honestly; misconceptions are normal and this just helps you notice what's about to click.

1. Which subatomic particle carries a negative charge?
Electrons are negative, protons are positive, and neutrons carry no charge at all — you'll use all three constantly in this lesson.
2. True or false: the number of protons in an atom can change without turning it into a different element.
False — the number of protons is the element's identity. Change that number and you've changed the element itself.
3. If a neutral atom loses an electron, what does it become?
Right idea to test — losing a negative electron leaves more protons than electrons, so the atom becomes positively charged: a cation.
4. True or false: two atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons.
True — this is exactly what an isotope is, and it's coming up soon.
5. What do you think "valence electrons" refers to?
Only the outermost shell counts — those are the electrons actually available to interact with other atoms, which makes them the single most important number in this whole lesson.
Concept 1 · Atoms & Subatomic Particles

What's actually inside an atom

Read the section below before touching the simulation — the three particles here are the foundation for everything else in this unit.

An atom is the smallest unit of an element that still counts as that element. Every atom is built from three subatomic particles. Protons carry a positive charge and sit packed inside the nucleus at the atom's center. Neutrons carry no charge at all and sit right alongside the protons in that same nucleus. Electrons carry a negative charge and don't sit in the nucleus at all — instead they occupy the space around it, arranged in shells (also called energy levels).

Protons and neutrons have almost identical mass — about 1 atomic mass unit (amu) each — and together they account for almost all of an atom's total mass. Electrons, by contrast, are almost weightless in comparison: roughly 1/1800th the mass of a proton. That means the tiny, dense nucleus holds nearly 100% of an atom's mass while taking up only a minuscule fraction of its total volume — nearly everything you'd think of as the "size" of an atom is really just empty space patrolled by electrons.

The number of protons in an atom is called its atomic number, and it's the single number that defines which element you're looking at — every carbon atom in the universe has exactly 6 protons, and nothing with 6 protons is ever anything other than carbon. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons always equals the number of protons, so the positive and negative charges cancel out to zero.

🏟️ The analogy

Picture a football stadium with a single marble sitting on the 50-yard line. That marble is the nucleus — tiny, but so dense it accounts for nearly all the "weight" of the whole stadium. Now picture a handful of gnats buzzing around in the very top row of seats, as far from the marble as possible. Those are the electrons: they take up almost all of the stadium's actual space, but weigh next to nothing by comparison.

Quick check: an atom has 6 protons and is electrically neutral. How many electrons does it have, and which element is it?

6 electrons — in a neutral atom, electrons always equal protons. And with 6 protons, this is carbon, no matter how many neutrons it happens to have.

Why this matters for biology: roughly 96% of your body's mass is built from just four elements — carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen — and each one is defined entirely by its proton count (6, 1, 8, and 7).

Concept 2 · Isotopes

Same element, different weight

Read carefully — this is where neutrons finally get their moment.

Every atom of a given element has the same number of protons — that's non-negotiable, since protons define the element. But the number of neutrons in that same atom can actually vary. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes. Because mass number is just protons plus neutrons added together, different isotopes of the same element have different masses even though they're chemically the same element.

Isotopes are usually written with the mass number attached, like carbon-12 or carbon-14. Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons (mass number 12); carbon-14 has the same 6 protons but 8 neutrons (mass number 14). Most isotopes are perfectly stable and just sit there being slightly heavier or lighter versions of the same element. Some, though, are radioactive — their nucleus is unstable and it slowly decays over time, releasing radiation as it does.

Here's the key idea to hold onto: since neutrons carry no charge and don't affect the electron shells at all, isotopes of the same element behave almost identically in chemical reactions. The chemistry of an atom comes from its protons and electrons — neutrons mostly just add weight.

🎒 The analogy

Imagine identical twins, same family, same face, same last name — except one of them is wearing a backpack stuffed with textbooks and the other isn't. From across the room, you'd still recognize them as the same family (same element). But if you put them on a scale, they'd read differently (different mass) purely because of the extra weight one of them happens to be carrying (extra neutrons).

Quick check: carbon-12 has 6 neutrons; carbon-14 has 8. Are they different elements? Why or why not?

No — both are still carbon. Element identity is set by the number of protons (6 in both cases), not neutrons. They're isotopes of the same element with different mass numbers, not different elements.

Why this matters for biology: carbon-14 decays at a slow, steady, predictable rate, which is exactly why scientists use it to determine the age of fossils and other once-living material — a technique called radiocarbon dating.

Concept 3 · Ions — Cations & Anions

When atoms gain or lose electrons

This is the concept that shows up everywhere once you get to how your nerves and muscles actually work — read closely.

An ion is an atom that no longer has equal numbers of protons and electrons, which means it carries an overall electric charge. Since the number of protons in an atom essentially never changes during ordinary chemistry, ions form when an atom gains or loses electrons — never protons.

An atom that loses one or more electrons ends up with more protons than electrons, giving it a net positive charge. This is called a cation. An atom that gains one or more electrons ends up with more electrons than protons, giving it a net negative charge. This is called an anion. A handy way to keep them straight: cations are positive (think of the "t" as a plus sign), and anions are negative.

Several ions show up constantly in biology: sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), and magnesium (Mg²⁺) are all cations, while chloride (Cl⁻) is a common anion. The little superscript number tells you exactly how many electrons were lost or gained — Ca²⁺ means calcium lost 2 electrons, not 1.

⚖️ The analogy

Picture an atom as a two-pan balance scale. Protons sit bolted to one pan — permanently fixed, never moving. Electrons sit on the other pan, free to hop on or off. When both pans hold equal numbers, the scale balances perfectly and the atom reads "neutral." Lift an electron off that pan, and the proton side now outweighs it — the scale tips positive, and you've got a cation. Drop an extra electron onto that pan instead, and it outweighs the protons — the scale tips negative, and you've got an anion. The protons never move; only the electron side of the scale ever changes.

Quick check: calcium normally has 20 protons and 20 electrons. In your bones and muscles it's almost always found as Ca²⁺. How many electrons does that ion actually have?

18 electrons. The 20 protons never change — calcium lost 2 electrons (20 − 2 = 18), leaving 2 more protons than electrons and a net charge of +2.

Why this matters for biology: the electrical signal that fires a neuron, contracts a muscle, or keeps your heart beating is really just ions — Na⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺ — flowing across cell membranes.

Concept 4 · Electron Distribution & Valence Electrons

Why electrons don't just pile up randomly

This concept sets up everything about how atoms bond — worth reading slowly.

Electrons don't swarm the nucleus randomly — they occupy specific energy levels, or shells, and they fill those shells from the inside out, lowest energy first. For the elements you'll deal with constantly in biology, a simplified rule works well: the first shell holds a maximum of 2 electrons, and the second and third shells each hold a maximum of 8. An atom always fills an inner shell completely before any electron moves to the next shell out.

The electrons sitting in an atom's outermost occupied shell are called its valence electrons. These are the only electrons that really matter for chemistry. Because they're the farthest from the nucleus, they're held the least tightly — which makes them the first (and often only) electrons available to interact with other atoms, whether that means being lost, gained, or shared.

One more detail worth knowing: a shell isn't just one big open space — within each shell, electrons actually occupy smaller regions called orbitals (for example, spherical s orbitals and dumbbell-shaped p orbitals), and each individual orbital holds a maximum of 2 electrons that pair up together. That pairing is exactly why the electron shell diagrams in this lesson group electrons two at a time instead of scattering them one by one around the ring.

This single number — how many valence electrons an atom has — is one of the most useful facts in all of chemistry. It's what determines whether an atom tends to lose electrons, gain electrons, or share them in a bond, which is the exact question you'll be predicting later in this lesson.

🧅 The analogy

Think of an atom like an onion sliced in half. The innermost layers are buried deep inside — they never touch your knife, your hand, or the cutting board. Only the outermost layer is actually exposed to the world outside the onion. Electrons work the same way: inner-shell electrons are buried too deep inside the atom to interact with anything else, but the electrons in the outermost occupied shell — the valence electrons — are the only ones exposed enough to touch a neighboring atom, which is exactly why they're the ones involved in every bond.

Quick check: sodium's electron distribution is 2, 8, 1. How many valence electrons does it have — and does that match an atom that tends to lose an electron?

Just 1 valence electron. An outer shell with only 1 electron is easy to give away entirely rather than fill up — which is exactly why sodium so readily forms Na⁺.

Why this matters for biology: valence electron count is the single number that predicts almost everything about how an atom bonds — it's the exact concept that connects straight into ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonding.

Post-assessment

These questions cover the same six topics as the pre-check, but from new angles — try to answer using what you just learned before checking each one.

1. Which two subatomic particles are located inside the nucleus?
Correct — protons and neutrons are packed together in the nucleus. Electrons live outside it, in the surrounding shells.
2. An atom has 12 protons and 12 neutrons. What is its mass number?
Right — mass number is protons plus neutrons added together (12 + 12 = 24). Electrons are essentially weightless, so they don't factor into it.
3. Which pair below are isotopes of each other?
Correct — matching protons (8) with different neutron counts (8 vs. 10) is exactly the definition of isotopes. The second pair has different protons (different elements), and the third pair has different electron counts (ions, not isotopes).
4. Phosphorus has an electron distribution of 2, 8, 5. How many valence electrons does it have, and what would you predict?
Right — the last number in the distribution is the valence count. With 5 out of 8 possible electrons, gaining 3 is more efficient than losing 5, so phosphorus tends to form an anion.
5. Scenario: A magnesium atom (normally 12 protons and 12 electrons) is found in a mineral sample with only 10 electrons. Which explanation best accounts for this?
Exactly — 12 protons always means magnesium. With 2 fewer electrons than protons (10 vs. 12), it carries a net +2 charge: this is Mg²⁺, an ion your body relies on for muscle and nerve function.
